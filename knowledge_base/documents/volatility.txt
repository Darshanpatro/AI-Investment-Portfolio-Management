Title: Volatility

Volatility is a statistical measure of the dispersion of returns for a
given asset or portfolio over a period of time. In practical terms, it
describes how much and how quickly the price of an investment moves up and
down. A highly volatile asset experiences large price swings in short
periods, while a low-volatility asset tends to have more stable, gradual
price changes.

Volatility is most commonly measured using the standard deviation of an
asset's returns. A higher standard deviation indicates that returns are
spread out over a wider range, implying greater unpredictability.

Volatility is not the same thing as risk, though the two are related.
Volatility describes the magnitude of price fluctuations, while risk more
broadly refers to the possibility of a permanent or significant loss.
Some investors view short-term volatility as an opportunity rather than a
danger, especially if they have a long time horizon and do not need to
sell during a downturn.

Sudden, unusual spikes in volatility compared to an asset's historical
pattern can sometimes be detected using anomaly detection techniques, such
as Isolation Forest, applied to daily returns. Such spikes do not, by
themselves, indicate the direction prices will move next; they simply
flag that recent behavior differs meaningfully from the historical norm
and may warrant closer attention.
