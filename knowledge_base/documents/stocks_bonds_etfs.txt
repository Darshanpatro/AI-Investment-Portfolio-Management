Title: Stocks, Bonds, and ETFs

A stock (also called equity or a share) represents partial ownership in a
company. When an investor buys a stock, they become a shareholder and are
entitled to a proportional share of the company's profits (often paid as
dividends) and, in principle, voting rights on major corporate decisions.
Stock prices fluctuate based on company performance, investor sentiment,
and broader market and economic conditions. Stocks are generally
considered higher risk and higher potential return compared to bonds.

A bond is a debt instrument. When an investor buys a bond, they are
effectively lending money to the issuer (a government or a corporation) in
exchange for periodic interest payments and the return of the principal
amount at maturity. Bonds are generally considered lower risk than stocks,
though they still carry risks such as interest rate risk (bond prices
generally fall when interest rates rise) and credit/default risk (the
issuer failing to repay).

An Exchange Traded Fund (ETF) is a pooled investment vehicle that holds a
basket of underlying assets, such as stocks, bonds, or commodities, and
trades on a stock exchange like an individual stock. ETFs are widely used
by investors seeking instant diversification, since a single ETF purchase
can provide exposure to dozens or hundreds of underlying securities. Many
ETFs track a specific index, such as a broad market index or a particular
sector, and typically have lower expense ratios than actively managed
mutual funds.

Choosing among stocks, bonds, and ETFs (or some mix of all three) is a
central part of asset allocation and depends heavily on an investor's risk
tolerance, investment horizon, and financial goals.
